    Grease Trap Inspection and Repair in Lacey

    An empty trap is the only time anyone can see what is wrong with it. Cracked baffles and missing outlet tees are the usual finds. Without an outlet tee, the grease cap flows right into the sewer, and older concrete interceptors lose that tee more often than you would think. Small fixes can often happen the same visit.

    Scheduled Grease Trap Maintenance Plans in Lacey

    Most pretreatment programs use the 25 percent rule: pump before floating grease and settled solids together take up a quarter of the trap's liquid depth. Other programs simply set a fixed interval, commonly 90 days for large interceptors. Whichever rule your program uses, we schedule to it. Changes are easy.

    Hydro-Jetting for Grease Lines in Lacey, WA

    A cable machine pokes a hole through grease. Jetting actually removes it. Our jetters run around 3,000 to 4,000 psi through a spinning nozzle, which scours the whole inside of the pipe and pushes the loosened grease downstream. Afterward a camera pass confirms the pipe is clean.

    Emergency Grease Trap Services in Lacey, WA

    Grease Line Jetting in Lacey

Snaking just bores a small path that closes up in weeks, but a rotating jetter nozzle strips grease off the full wall of the pipe. A camera confirms it afterward.

    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in Lacey

    Most visits in Lacey run somewhere between $200 and $800, set mostly by trap size and how long it has gone since the last pump-out. Hard access adds time. A trap behind a walk-in that needs 80 feet of hose costs more than one sitting next to the curb. You get the price first.

    Zurn, Schier, Watts and Big Dipper units turn up across Washington every week, along with Rockford traps and older steel boxes from makers that are long gone. Parts are usually available. For discontinued units we can often fit a universal baffle or flow control, or quote a replacement if the body has rusted through.

    Staff can clean a small under-sink trap between pump-outs. The waste still needs legal disposal. A common setup is daily skimming by staff and a full pump and scrape from us every few weeks, so the records stay complete.

    Everything in the trap comes out, from the floating grease cap down to the sludge of food solids on the floor. The industry calls it brown grease. It is different from the yellow grease in your used fryer oil bin, which is recycled separately and should never be poured into the trap.

    Under-sink traps take 30 to 60 minutes. Outdoor interceptors run one to three hours. Big tanks with a hardened cap take the longest, since the cap has to be broken up with a pressure wand before the hose will pull it.

    FOG stands for fats, oils and grease. It pours as a liquid and sets up hard once it cools. Inside a sewer pipe it builds up in layers on the walls, traps food and wipes, and can shrink the opening to the size of a pencil.

    It depends on trap size and menu. Most restaurants end up on a two-week to three-month interval. Homes with a small unit may go a year, but a fryer-heavy kitchen on a 50-gallon trap might need pumping every other week.

    No permit is needed just to have a trap pumped. The records are your responsibility. A new or resized trap is different, usually needing a plumbing permit and sometimes a review by the pretreatment program first.
