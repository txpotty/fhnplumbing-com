    Small under-sink traps often start around $200 a visit. Large outdoor interceptors can pass $1,000 when they are full and the cap has hardened. The biggest variable is the gallons hauled, because disposal facilities charge by volume and a 1,500-gallon tank is a full truckload.

    It is the most common pumping standard. When grease and solids reach a quarter of the depth, it is time to pump. Beyond that level, water passes through too quickly to separate, and grease leaves through the outlet even though the trap seems fine.

    Skimming off the top layer yourself is usually allowed. Disposing of it is where people get in trouble. Pretreatment programs often require a licensed hauler's record for each pump-out, so a kitchen that handles its own cleaning may have no paperwork to show when the inspector asks.

    Grease passes the outlet and plugs the line. Backups follow, usually at the worst hour of service. Beyond the mess, many sewer districts fine kitchens for grease discharges and can bill them for jetting the public main, and a health inspector can close a kitchen with sewage on the floor.

    Short term, slow drains and smell. Leave it long enough and the grease cap sets up like a brick. Hydrogen sulfide from the rotting grease also attacks concrete, so interceptors that go unpumped for years often need new baffles and risers well before their time.

    A small under-sink trap can be hand-cleaned by staff. The waste still has to go somewhere legal. In most places you cannot put trap waste in the dumpster or pour it down another drain, and outdoor interceptors need a vacuum truck and a licensed hauler no matter what.

    FOG stands for fats, oils and grease. It pours as a liquid and sets up hard once it cools. Inside a sewer pipe it builds up in layers on the walls, traps food and wipes, and can shrink the opening to the size of a pencil.
