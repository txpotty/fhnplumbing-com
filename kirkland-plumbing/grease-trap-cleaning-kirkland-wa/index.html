    Small under-sink traps often start around $200 a visit. Large outdoor interceptors can pass $1,000 when they are full and the cap has hardened. The biggest variable is the gallons hauled, because disposal facilities charge by volume and a 1,500-gallon tank is a full truckload.

    Staff can clean a small under-sink trap between pump-outs. The waste still needs legal disposal. A common setup is daily skimming by staff and a full pump and scrape from us every few weeks, so the records stay complete.

    Skimming off the top layer yourself is usually allowed. The disposal is the part that gets people fined. Many programs want a licensed hauler's record for every cleaning, and a kitchen doing its own has nothing to show the inspector.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Most people use trap for the small indoor unit, usually under 100 gallons. An interceptor is the big in-ground tank outside, often 500 gallons or more, and plumbing codes treat them as separate categories with different sizing rules.

    It goes to a permitted receiving facility. Some facilities process it for biofuel or digesters. What is left gets dewatered and disposed of under the facility's permit, and each load is logged from your trap to their gate.

    Under-sink hydromechanical traps and in-ground gravity interceptors make up most of our work. Automatic grease removal units are on the list too. Those AGRUs have a heater and a skimmer wheel that pull grease into a container, and they need the container emptied and the wheel and screen cleaned on a schedule.

    Slow sinks come first. Then gurgling floor drains and a sour smell near the dish area. By the time gray water is coming up through a floor sink, the trap is usually well past 25 percent full and grease is already heading down the line.
