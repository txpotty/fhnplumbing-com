    Underground Pipe Damage: Common Causes and Modern Repair Solutions

    By Luis Ortega · August 31, 2026 · 5 min read

    What Goes Wrong Under the Yard

    Your sewer lateral and water service run from the house to the street, usually somewhere 2 to 6 feet underground. They stay out of mind until the lawn gets soggy or a toilet backs up. Most laterals are 4-inch, and on older houses that usually means clay or cast iron. Each fails in its own way.

    Common Causes

    Tree Roots

    Roots find sewer lines by following moisture from a cracked joint or a loose gasket. Once a hair-thin root gets in, it grows into a mat that catches paper and grease. Clay pipe is the worst for this. The joints every two or three feet give roots a way in, and a mature oak or maple can fill a 4-inch line in a few seasons.

    Shifting Soil

    Glacial till and dense hardpan are common in western Washington, and they drain poorly enough to keep water against foundations and in crawlspaces.

    Soil that settles or swells moves the pipe with it. Rigid pipe like clay or cast iron does not bend, so it either cracks or separates at a joint. Sags are common too. A belly in the line holds standing water and waste, and the camera shows it as a section that stays full after a flush.

    Old Pipe Material

    Housing in Washington ranges from early 1900s homes to new construction, with plumbing materials that change a lot from one decade to the next.

    Age wears every material down. Cast iron rusts from the inside, and the bottom of the pipe can corrode through while the top still looks solid. Orangeburg pipe, used through the early 1970s, is pressed tar paper that slowly flattens into an oval. Galvanized water service lines close up with rust and lose pressure at every faucet.

    Signs of a Buried Line Problem

    Several drains slowing at once points to the main line. One classic sign is a toilet that gurgles as the washer empties. In the yard, watch for a strip of grass that is greener than the rest, or ground that feels soft over the line during dry weather. Sewer smell near the cleanout is another.

    On the water side, the meter tells the story. With everything in the house shut off, watch the small leak triangle or dial on the meter. When that indicator spins, water is escaping between the meter and the house, often from the buried service. Low pressure throughout the house can mean the same.

    A sewer camera settles it. We run a camera through the cleanout and record the whole line, with a locator that marks depth and position on the ground above. We show you the footage as it plays. The footage decides the repair.

    Modern Repair Options

    Trench repair is still the right call for some jobs. A collapsed section or a line with a deep belly usually has to be dug up, since lining needs a pipe that still holds its shape. Spot repairs can be small. One cracked joint might only need a four-by-six hole and a day on site.

    Trenchless lining avoids most of the digging. A felt liner soaked in epoxy resin is pulled into the line, inflated, and cured into a pipe within the pipe. Most residential liners go in through the cleanout and one small access pit, and the job takes a day. Roots cannot get through it.

    Pipe bursting is the other trenchless method. A bursting head pulled through the old line breaks it apart and drags new HDPE pipe into place right behind it. You need a pit on each end, but the lawn in between stays untouched. Our crews at FHN Plumbing use both methods across Washington, depending on what the camera shows.

    When to Call

    Do not wait on a sewer line that has backed up twice. Every backup leaves more waste behind, and that feeds the roots. A typical camera inspection takes less than an hour, and we hand you the video along with the marked location of the damage. Reach FHN Plumbing at (425) 491-3131. Ask about the cleanout location when you call, since an accessible cleanout saves time.
