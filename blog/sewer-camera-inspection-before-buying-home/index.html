    Why a Sewer Camera Inspection Belongs on Every Washington Home Inspection

    By Andrew Blake · August 13, 2026 · 5 min read

    A general home inspection covers a lot. The inspector checks the roof, the electrical panel, the HVAC and the water heater, plus the visible plumbing. What most of them do not do is look inside the main sewer line that runs from the house to the street or septic tank. Running water in every fixture for 20 minutes proves very little, because a cracked or root-packed line can drain fine during a short test. A sewer scope is one of the best add-ons a buyer can order before closing.

    What a Sewer Camera Inspection Involves

    The plumber feeds a waterproof camera on a flexible push cable through a cleanout, usually near the foundation or out in the front yard. The video records as the camera moves down the pipe, and you can watch it live on the monitor. A transmitter at the camera head lets us mark the spot and depth of any problem from the surface. Plan on 30 to 60 minutes. Ask for the video file and a written summary before the plumber leaves.

    No cleanout? We can sometimes go in from a roof vent or through a toilet flange. Either way it adds a bit to the bill. Most residential scopes run $150 to $350 when there is a cleanout in reach.

    Before the visit, ask the listing agent for any records of past sewer work. Root cutting invoices or an old lining warranty are worth having. That history tells the plumber what to look for and whether earlier repairs are holding up. Sellers often have it in a drawer.

    Ask the seller, too, whether the house has ever had a main line backup into the basement or the lowest shower. If the answer is yes, we slow down near the foundation, where most of those backups start. Honest answers save time.

    What the Camera Can Reveal

    Sewer problems are expensive, and you rarely see them from inside the house. A camera can turn up:

    • Root intrusion at the joints, common near mature trees.
    • Bellies or sags where the pipe settled and waste collects.
    • Offset or separated joints from years of soil movement.
    • Cracked or collapsed sections in clay tile or cast iron.
    • Heavy corrosion and scale inside older cast iron.
    • Grease buildup that points to ongoing maintenance.
    • Bad past repairs, such as mismatched pipe joined without proper couplings.

    Why It Matters for Washington Buyers

    Housing in Washington ranges from early 1900s homes to new construction, with plumbing materials that change a lot from one decade to the next.

    Every era of construction brings its own sewer risks. Older neighborhoods may still run on the original clay tile or cast iron. A lot of houses from the 1960s through the 1980s have cast iron under the slab that rots from the inside. Newer ABS and PVC is not immune either. Expansive soil and poor bedding still cause bellies and pulled joints.

    Trees add another wrinkle. Shade trees help curb appeal, but their roots go hunting for moisture in the sewer. Rural lots and some edge-of-town properties use septic instead. There the camera shows the condition of the line out to the tank, which pairs well with a separate septic inspection.

    Septic systems are common in rural and some suburban parts of Washington, and many counties require regular inspections of on-site systems.

    How the Results Help You Negotiate

    Sewer repairs cost real money. A spot repair can run $1,500 to $4,500, while a new lateral may cost $4,000 to $12,000 or more. Finding a problem before closing gives you options. The seller can make the repair or give you a credit, or you can decide the house is not the one. If the line looks good, you get a baseline video to compare against later. Sellers benefit too, since a pre-listing scope lets them fix problems on their own schedule.

    When to Call a Professional

    Book the scope early in your inspection contingency period. That leaves time to get repair quotes if the video shows trouble. A licensed plumber, not a general inspector, is the right person for this, because we can read the footage, pinpoint each defect and price the real fix.
