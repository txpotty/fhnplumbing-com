    Slab Leak Warning Signs Every Washington Homeowner Should Know

    By Aaron Mitchell · October 15, 2026 · 5 min read

    What a Slab Leak Actually Is

    A lot of houses built since the 1960s sit on a poured concrete slab, with copper or PEX supply lines and cast iron or PVC drains running through or under the concrete. There is nothing wrong with building a house that way. A slab leak starts when one of those buried lines develops a pinhole, a crack or a failed joint, and water starts moving into the concrete or the soil below it. Some leak a cup a day, others leak gallons an hour.

    Crawlspaces and basements are both common in Washington homes, and a wet crawlspace is often the first sign of a drainage or plumbing problem.

    Soil under any slab swells when it gets wet and shrinks when it dries, and that cycle shifts the concrete by fractions of an inch over the years. Buried pipe handles that movement poorly. Copper rubs against rebar or gravel, joints get pulled, and a line that was fine for twenty years gives out one ordinary afternoon.

    Warning Signs Worth Taking Seriously

    A Water Bill That Jumps for No Reason

    Pull up the last six months of bills and compare gallons, not dollars. If usage climbed and your habits did not, something is leaking. A pinhole in a 3/4-inch copper line under normal house pressure can push out enough water in a day to fill a hot tub, and the meter will show it long before you see a drop anywhere in the house. Check the meter with every fixture off.

    Warm Spots on the Floor

    Walk the house barefoot. When a hot line leaks under the slab, the concrete above it heats up, and you will feel a patch of tile or laminate that stays warm while the rest of the floor is cool. Pets find these spots first. A thermal imaging camera shows the leak path in a few minutes.

    Running Water With Every Tap Closed

    Late at night, the house finally goes quiet. Put your ear near the floor in a hall or closet. A hiss or faint rushing sound with every faucet, toilet and appliance shut off usually means pressurized water is escaping somewhere under your feet. A toilet that cycles on its own is a different issue, usually a worn flapper.

    Pressure That Drops Off

    Weak flow at one faucet is usually a clogged aerator. Weak flow everywhere is a different story. When a supply line under the slab is broken, part of the water headed for your shower is going into the dirt instead, so pressure sags across the whole house and gets worse when two fixtures run at once. A screw-on gauge at the hose bib tells us the static pressure in seconds.

    New Cracks in Walls or Floors

    Water moving under a slab washes out fine soil and softens clay, and over a few months the concrete above it can settle unevenly. The signs show up as cracked grout, drywall splits above door frames and gaps opening under the baseboards. Doors that used to close fine start sticking. At that point the leak is also a foundation problem.

    Mold, Mildew and Musty Carpet

    A musty smell in one room, or carpet pad that stays damp along an interior wall, is a common sign that a slab leak has been wicking moisture up through the concrete for a while. Mildew tends to appear low on the drywall and behind the trim. Cleaning it without finding the water only buys a few weeks.

    Soggy Spots Outside the Foundation

    Some leaks push water sideways until it surfaces. Look for a strip of grass along the foundation that stays green in a dry spell. Puddles that never dry, soil pulling away from the footing, or a flower bed that is always mud can all trace back to a line under the house.

    When to Call a Professional

    One warning sign by itself can be a coincidence. Two or more together usually are not. Before you reach FHN Plumbing at (425) 491-3131, shut off every fixture, read the water meter, wait an hour without running anything and read it again. If the numbers moved, the leak is on the pressurized side.

    Why Detection Gear Matters

    Finding a slab leak is a locating job before it is a repair job. We use acoustic listening gear, a thermal camera, an isolation pressure test and a line tracer to mark the leak within a foot or so. That precision matters because the repair options, a spot repair through the slab or a reroute through the walls and ceilings, both depend on knowing exactly where the break sits and what kind of pipe it is. Guessing means more broken concrete.

    Rerouting is often the better call on older copper. A copper line that has sprung one pinhole after 40 years usually has more coming. Running new PEX overhead skips the concrete entirely, and most homes are back on water the same day or the next, with a few drywall patches as the only cleanup left behind.
