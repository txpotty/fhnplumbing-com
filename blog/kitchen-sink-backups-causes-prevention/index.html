    Kitchen Sink Backups: Causes, Prevention and Quick Solutions

    By Dana Whitfield · September 9, 2026 · 5 min read

    Why Kitchen Sinks Back Up

    The kitchen drain takes more abuse than any other drain in the house. Grease and starch wash down warm, then cool and cling to the inside of the pipe. In a year or two, that layer can shrink a 1-1/2 inch drain to about the width of a finger. Then one big rinse finds the limit.

    The Usual Causes

    Grease is the main one. Bacon fat and cooking oil both harden once they cool. Chasing grease with hot water just moves it a few feet down the line, where it hardens in the run behind the wall. Wipe pans with a paper towel first.

    Garbage disposals grind food, but they do not make it disappear. Coffee grounds and eggshells settle in the trap like wet sand. Fibrous stuff such as celery and corn husks wraps around the disposal blades or forms a thick mat down in the trap. Pasta and rice swell after they go down.

    Soft water can be mildly corrosive to copper over time, which sometimes shows up as pinhole leaks in older supply lines.

    Hard water leaves scale inside older galvanized and cast iron drains, and that rough surface catches grease faster than smooth PVC. Houses built before the 1970s often still have galvanized drain arms in the kitchen wall. Those pipes rust closed from the inside. Chemical cleaners will not keep them open for long.

    Dishwasher and Disposal Connections

    A sink that backs up when the dishwasher drains is a clue. The dishwasher pushes several gallons fast, and a partial clog downstream sends it right back up into the sink basin. A new disposal with the knockout plug still in place will do the same thing. Check that first after a disposal swap.

    Keeping the Line Clear

    Prevention here is cheap. Pour cooled grease into a can and throw it out when it sets. Use plenty of cold water with the disposal, and let it run about 15 seconds after the motor quiets down. A basket strainer catches the rest.

    Once a month, flush the line. Fill both sink bowls with hot water, then pull the plugs at the same time. The sudden volume scours the trap and the drain arm behind the wall much better than a running faucet ever will. Skip boiling water if you have PVC drains, since repeated boiling water can soften the joints. Hot tap water is plenty.

    Quick Fixes for a Minor Clog

    Start with a cup plunger, not the flange type used on toilets. On a double sink, plug the other drain with a wet rag or a second stopper, or the plunger just pushes air out the other side. Ten or fifteen firm strokes is usually enough. Leave the dishwasher off until it drains.

    Next, pull the trap. With a bucket underneath, hand-loosen the two slip nuts and clear the U-bend. A lot of clogs are right there, in the first foot of pipe. A clean trap means the clog is in the wall, and a quarter-inch hand auger is the next tool to reach for.

    Skip chemical drain cleaners. Most will not cut through grease, and when they fail they sit in the trap. That leaves caustic water for whoever opens the line next, and it can damage older metal drains and the finish on some sinks. Enzyme cleaners are fine for maintenance.

    When to Bring in a Plumber

    Water rising in another fixture changes things. A tub that gurgles or a floor drain that bubbles as the sink drains puts the blockage in the main line rather than the kitchen branch. That takes a cable machine through a cleanout, or a hydro jetter for heavy grease. Do not keep running water.
