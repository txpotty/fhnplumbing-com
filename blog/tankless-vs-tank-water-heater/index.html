    Tankless vs. Tank Water Heaters: What Works Best in Washington

    By Travis Baines · September 18, 2026 · 6 min read

    Two Ways to Heat Water

    A tank heater keeps 40 to 80 gallons hot around the clock. Tankless heaters fire up when you open a faucet and shut off when you close it. Both can be gas or electric, both can serve a whole house, and the choice usually comes down to how your family uses hot water and what your gas line and water will support. Neither is the right answer everywhere.

    The Tank Heater

    Tanks are simple and cheap to replace. A standard 50-gallon gas tank swap is usually a same-day job. The tradeoff is standby loss, since the burner or elements cycle all day to keep stored water hot whether anyone is home or not, and the tank holds a fixed amount, so the fourth shower in a row runs cold. Most tanks last somewhere between 8 and 12 years.

    Tanks also forgive a lot. They tolerate a small gas line and moderate scale without complaint. If the power goes out, a gas tank with a standing pilot keeps making hot water, and the stored 50 gallons can even serve as an emergency supply drawn from the drain valve. That counts for something during a storm.

    Housing in Washington ranges from early 1900s homes to new construction, with plumbing materials that change a lot from one decade to the next.

    The Tankless Heater

    Tankless heaters are rated by flow, in gallons per minute at a given temperature rise. The bigger gas models top out around 8 to 9 gallons a minute on mild incoming water. Colder incoming water means a bigger temperature rise, so the same unit delivers less flow in winter, and two showers plus a dishwasher can push a small unit past what it can keep up with. Get the size right first.

    The upside is real. Endless hot water is the headline, as long as the unit is sized for your peak demand. Without standby loss, a gas tankless unit typically burns noticeably less fuel than a tank, it hangs on a wall instead of eating closet floor space, and a well-kept unit can run 20 years. Most of that life depends on the heat exchanger staying clean.

    Maintenance looks different on each. A tank wants a flush once a year through the drain valve to clear sediment. The anode rod, a sacrificial magnesium or aluminum rod that keeps the steel from rusting, should be checked every three to five years, and replacing a spent anode is one of the cheapest ways to get extra years out of a tank. Tankless units need descaling and an inlet screen cleaning.

    What Changes the Math in a Washington Home

    Municipal water west of the mountains in Washington is typically soft, while supplies on the drier east side tend to run harder.

    Installation is where tankless costs climb. Most whole-house gas units need a 3/4-inch gas line and stainless or PVC venting, depending on the model. Electric tankless models for a whole house can need 120 amps or more on dedicated breakers, which many older panels simply do not have room for, so a panel upgrade sometimes rides along with the job. We look at the panel and the gas meter first.

    Hard water is the other big variable. Mineral buildup on the heat exchanger cuts flow and throws fault codes. On hard water, plan to descale the unit every year with a small pump and a descaling solution circulated through the isolation valves, or put a softener ahead of it. A tank collects the same minerals as sediment on the bottom.

    Constant moisture west of the mountains promotes mold, rot and corrosion around leaks, so small drips should not be ignored.

    Family size and habits decide the rest. Two people seldom need more than one hot tap at a time. A family of five with teenagers and a soaking tub is a different load entirely, and that house needs either a large tankless unit sized for three fixtures at once or a bigger tank with a fast recovery rate. We ask how many showers run at the same time.

    Code details matter on either type. A tank needs a temperature and pressure relief valve piped to a safe discharge point. Gas units of both kinds need proper combustion air and venting, and a tankless unit needs isolation valves so it can be flushed without cutting into the plumbing every year. Inspectors check every one of those.

    Ask about recovery rate when comparing tanks. Gas tanks recover a lot quicker than electric tanks of the same size. First-hour rating, printed on the yellow energy label, tells you how many gallons a tank delivers in the first hour of heavy use, and it is a better number to compare than tank size alone for a busy house. Heat pump water heaters sit in between, with lower running costs and slower recovery.

    When to Call a Professional

    A replacement is the cheapest time to switch. A tank past 10 years or weeping at the base is worth pricing both ways now.
