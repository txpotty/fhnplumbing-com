    Commercial Kitchen Grease Trap Installs in Shoreline

    Every commercial install starts with a fixture list. We count each sink, floor drain and dish machine that will tie in, then add the gallons per minute each one can dump. From there we pick a hydromechanical unit or a gravity tank and figure out where it can sit with a straight shot to the building sewer. Fewer bends, fewer clogs.

    Grease Trap Replacement and Upgrades in Shoreline

    Plastic and steel traps both wear out, just in different ways. With steel, rust eats the seams and the welded baffles first. Plastic cracks. When we pull an old trap we cap the lines, haul it out, set the new unit on the same footprint where possible and reconnect with no-hub couplings so the next swap goes faster.

    Zurn Grease Trap Installation in Shoreline

    Zurn's indoor grease interceptors cover the common sizes from small under-sink units up to 100 GPM, and we set enough of them to know where the gaskets give trouble. The lid gasket is the usual leak point after a few years. We keep spares on the truck. Most Zurn units come with a flow control fitting, and we mount it on the inlet side at the height the instructions call for.

    Automatic Grease Removal Units in Shoreline

    An automatic grease removal unit heats the trapped grease and skims it into a container on a timer, so staff empty a bucket instead of scraping a tank. The purchase price is higher than a passive trap. Plan for a dedicated circuit near the unit, which your electrician will need to run. Fry-heavy kitchens like them most.

    Hydro-Mechanical Grease Trap Installation in Shoreline, WA

    Hydromechanical traps use baffles and a vented flow control to pull grease out of water moving at a set rate, usually 20 to 100 gallons per minute. They fit indoors, under a sink or in a floor pit. The flow control is not optional. Skip it and a full sink drain overruns the rating, which sends grease right past the baffles. We look for units certified to PDI G-101 or ASME A112.14.3.

    Hydro-Mechanical Grease Interceptor Installation in Shoreline

    Inside a hydromechanical interceptor, baffles do the separating while a flow control fitting with an air intake holds incoming water to the rated flow. The air intake has to be vented to code, not left open in the kitchen. Get that wrong and the trap siphons. We also set the unit level within a quarter inch, since a tilted trap short-circuits the baffles and loses capacity.

    Grease Trap Brands We Install in Shoreline

    Zurn, Watts, Schier and Endura cover most of what we set in commercial kitchens, plus precast concrete tanks for the big outdoor interceptors. Each brand has its own lid gasket and baffle layout. Parts availability matters. We lean toward units with parts a local supply house keeps on the shelf, so a cracked lid does not close you for a week.

    Food Truck Commissary and Ghost Kitchen Traps in Shoreline, WA

    Commissary kitchens see a dozen trucks dumping wash water in the same two hours, which is a different load from a restaurant spreading it over a long shift. Sizing has to cover that short, heavy spike. Ghost kitchens with four or five brands under one roof often share a single trap, so the fixture count can be higher than the floor plan suggests. Each brand's prep sink counts.

    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in Shoreline

    Expect roughly $1,500 to $4,000 for a small indoor trap and $7,000 to $20,000 or more for an outdoor gravity interceptor. Recessing an indoor unit into the slab pushes the price up because of saw-cutting and patching. Location drives the rest. A tank under a parking lot needs a traffic-rated lid and asphalt repair, which a tank in a grass strip does not.

    We start with a fixture list and the drain rate of each fixture. Sinks are calculated by compartment volume and drain-down time, typically one or two minutes, and dish machines by the manufacturer's discharge rating. That gives the GPM figure. Hydromechanical traps are picked by that GPM rating, while gravity interceptors are sized by volume and retention time from the same data.

    Zurn and Watts make up a lot of our indoor installs, with Schier and Endura when a plastic body makes more sense. For outdoor work we set precast concrete tanks from nearby casting yards, or polyethylene interceptors when weight or access is a problem. Any certified brand works. We skip uncertified units, since inspectors turn them down.

    As close to the grease-producing fixtures as the space allows, so grease does not cool and coat the pipe before it reaches the trap. Leave room to open the lid and clean it without moving equipment. Never under a cook line. Outdoor interceptors go where a pump truck can park within reach of the hose and where the lids will not end up under a dumpster or a parked car.

    Indoor hydromechanical traps and outdoor gravity interceptors cover nearly every job we do. Automatic grease removal units are a pricier option for kitchens that fry all day. Most cafes go hydromechanical. High-volume kitchens usually get an outdoor gravity tank, especially if the sewer authority asks for one.

    Half a day for most indoor units. That covers setting the trap, piping the inlet and outlet, installing the flow control and running a water test. Outdoor tanks need two to five days, with an inspection before backfill. Permits add lead time.
