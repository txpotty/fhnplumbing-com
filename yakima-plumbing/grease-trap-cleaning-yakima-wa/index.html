    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in Yakima

    Small under-sink traps often start around $200 a visit. A large interceptor with a hardened grease cap can run past $1,000. The biggest variable is the gallons hauled, because disposal facilities charge by volume and a 1,500-gallon tank is a full truckload.

    Skimming off the top layer yourself is usually allowed. Disposing of it is where people get in trouble. Pretreatment programs often require a licensed hauler's record for each pump-out, so a kitchen that handles its own cleaning may have no paperwork to show when the inspector asks.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Generally a trap is the small unit indoors, often under 100 gallons. An interceptor is the big in-ground tank outside, often 500 gallons or more, and plumbing codes treat them as separate categories with different sizing rules.

    Staff can clean a small under-sink trap between pump-outs. Disposal still has to be legal. A common setup is daily skimming by staff and a full pump and scrape from us every few weeks, so the records stay complete.

    A small under-sink trap can be hand-cleaned by staff. Disposal is the hard part. In most places you cannot put trap waste in the dumpster or pour it down another drain, and outdoor interceptors need a vacuum truck and a licensed hauler no matter what.

    Zurn, Schier, Watts and Big Dipper units turn up across Washington every week, along with Rockford traps and older steel boxes from makers that are long gone. Parts are usually available. On discontinued models, a universal baffle or flow control often fits, and if the steel has rusted through we quote a replacement.
